Learning to See. Empowered to Transform.

Are we the integration layer?

Imagine you're investigating a vendor compliance claim. You need one delivery reference to move forward.

You leave the case, open SAP, find the right transaction, run a query, export the results to Excel, locate the information, and copy it back into the claim. Then you pick up where you left off—until the next piece of information sends you somewhere else.

Each step makes sense on its own. Together, they consume time, interrupt concentration, and pull skilled people away from the work that actually requires their judgment.

On a warehouse floor, we would notice that movement. We would see the extra walking, study why it happens, and consider moving what people need closer to where they work. On a screen, the same movement leaves no worn path. It can remain invisible for years because the people doing the work have become so good at navigating it.

Being good at working around a problem doesn't mean the problem should remain.

That realization is at the heart of how I think about Continuous Improvement—and of what our team set out to accomplish with Arvato Case Execution, or ACE.

When Lean thinking meets digital possibility

As a young shadow engineer at Toyota, I learned to see waste and value in the everyday movement of work. Years of driving Lean and Continuous Improvement across different organizations reinforced something important: the processes, challenges, and paths through which value flows can look entirely different, but the underlying purpose doesn't change. We want to make work better for the people doing it, improve quality, and create greater value for those we serve.

Later, my years in engineering consulting introduced me to digital tools, data, and automation capabilities that fundamentally expanded what I thought was possible. The Lean mindset hadn't changed. What changed was our ability to act on what we could see.

Lean taught me to see what was limiting us. Technology gave us new ways to challenge what was possible.

That combination shapes how our Continuous Improvement team works in the US. We bring Process Improvement and Digital Improvement together, working closely with operational leaders, client teams, and the people closest to the process. We start by understanding the work and the opportunity—not by choosing a technology—and use both disciplines to create a better way forward.

ACE is an example of what that approach looks like in practice.

It's the value stream that matters

That approach came to life when we began examining Harman's vendor compliance process.

Our teams were managing information from more than ten retailer portals and working through over thirty spreadsheets each week. Data had to be collected, transformed, shared, reviewed, investigated, and reconciled across people, systems, and organizations. Knowledgeable colleagues had developed elaborate ways to keep everything moving.

Working closely with the client and account teams, we mapped that work from beginning to end. We followed the information, the decisions, and the handoffs. We asked what each step contributed and where effort was being spent simply moving work from one place to another.

The mapping showed just how much valuable expertise was being spent bridging gaps between systems and processes.

People knew which portal contained the right information, how to translate one reference into another, which spreadsheet needed updating, and whom to contact when something didn't line up. Their knowledge was holding the process together.

In effect, our people had become the integration layer between disconnected systems, information, and processes. They were doing the work our technology should have been helping them do.

Our people shouldn't need to understand the complexity of our systems to benefit from their capabilities.

That changed the question we were trying to answer. Rather than making each individual activity faster, we wanted to remove unnecessary activities and make the entire flow work better. Because the process crossed the client–Arvato boundary, improving either side in isolation would still leave some of the handoffs untouched.

With ACE, we approached the design around the case and the decisions it requires, with shared context, clear ownership, relevant evidence, and appropriate access controls.

The value stream doesn't stop at an organizational boundary. Our improvement thinking shouldn't stop there either.

Making our systems work for our people

ACE is built on Microsoft Dynamics 365 Customer Service, supported by Power Platform, automation, and integrations. But the most interesting part of its design is how it puts capabilities we already have to work together.

Consider that delivery-reference lookup.

A retailer may identify a shipment using a reference different from the one in Arvato's systems. FIREWORKS, through its data services and curated semantic models, can provide the targeted information needed to connect those references. RALF provides access to supporting documents, including proof of delivery. ACE brings that information into the case alongside the process guidance needed to investigate and resolve it.

What once involved leaving the claim, finding information in another system, exporting it, and transferring it back can now be supported through an integrated retrieval experience.

The value isn't simply the clicks saved. It's the uninterrupted flow of work—and the attention given back to the person making the decision.

Instead of asking people to connect the dots between our systems, we connect the systems around our people.

We applied the same thinking to expertise. Business process flows make the next step, ownership, and requirements visible. Configurable business rules are already in use, allowing authorized business owners to adjust supported conditions and routing without turning every change into a development project. That makes routine execution more consistent while preserving human judgment for exceptions and more complex decisions.

The ACE Copilot Agent is also live. Team members can ask questions such as "Show me my active cases," request actions such as "Assign case #CA-554-01," and get summaries of case activities and notes. The aim is to make case information and everyday actions more accessible within the flow of work.

The next step, planned for the first quarter of 2027, is an AI-supported knowledge cycle: capturing lessons from resolved cases, validating them, and making that learning available to help people handle future cases. Unlike the Copilot Agent and configurable rules, this knowledge cycle is planned rather than live today.

Our goal is to make specialized knowledge more accessible, while allowing experienced colleagues to spend more of their time on the exceptions, relationships, and decisions where they add the greatest value.

The sophistication of our technology should make people's work simpler, not make their lives more complicated.

What changed—and what comes next

The performance results give this story a concrete foundation.

The vendor compliance process had a historical average resolution time of 16 days, and the contractual 10-day service-level target had not been consistently achieved over the preceding three years.

In July 2026, the team reported 1,951 claims closed, with an average resolution time of 6.2 days, with claim volume 168% higher than the previous month (June 2026). In August 2026, the average fell to 3.8 days, with 100% of claims completed within the 10-day SLA.

Those outcomes are significant. They reflect what can happen when process design, technology, customer partnership, and day-to-day execution move in the same direction. They don't, by themselves, tell us exactly how much each individual change contributed, and we shouldn't pretend otherwise.

Just as importantly, structured work gives us better visibility into recurring issues and the conditions that create them. Faster claim resolution is valuable. Learning how to prevent avoidable claims is an even better opportunity.

That's what a Continuous Improvement mindset makes possible: each result becomes a starting point for a better question.

Innovation grows when we build on each other

ACE also demonstrates something important about how we innovate as an organization.

FIREWORKS and RALF were developed by other teams to solve important problems of their own. Our team could build on those capabilities instead of starting from scratch. Their earlier work opened possibilities that weren't necessarily part of the original design.

This is the kind of collaboration we want to strengthen across Arvato—not only between technologies, but between sites, functions, client teams, and people who understand our work from different perspectives. It grows through relationships where asking questions, sharing discoveries, and offering help become natural.

There will be good reasons to have multiple applications or implementations. Customer requirements, security, and operating conditions aren't always the same. But the discoveries we make while solving a problem—what worked, what failed, the design patterns and lessons that took time to uncover—shouldn't have to be rediscovered by every team.

The greatest value in sharing a solution may be the learning that makes the next one possible.

That's how digital innovation begins to compound rather than remain a collection of individual projects.

The part you can't put on a dashboard

The work wasn't always easy, and we didn't get every detail right the first time.

Being willing to share our failures and learn from them is an important part of this story. When you change how people do their jobs, you also change routines they've depended on. Some ideas that seem clear during design become more complicated when someone has to live with them every day. Listening to understand those experiences—and acting on what we learn—is an essential part of improvement.

It's also how teams build trust. People begin to recognize their own experiences reflected in the solution, and they see that speaking up can make a difference. That is why improvement work cannot end at launch. The conversations after a solution goes live can teach us as much as the discovery that came before it.

A technically sound application is one achievement. A team that feels ownership of a better way of working is something more lasting.

Trust grows when people feel heard. It becomes stronger when they see their feedback lead to meaningful change.

A note of gratitude

ACE was a collaborative effort across functions, teams, and organizations. Many people contributed to its success, and a few relationships were especially important in making it possible.

My manager, Andreas Podwojewski, Managing Director for North America and Brazil, consistently challenges our teams and organization to find new ways of working. He leads with a growth mindset, encourages us to think bigger, and supports us in turning those ambitions into results. I'm especially grateful for his commitment to Continuous Improvement, his personal encouragement, and the trust he placed in me to lead our teams on this journey. His confidence in our ability to get across the finish line was a source of motivation throughout.

Erica Dunn, Arvato Account Manager for Harman, leads through action and integrity, living the behaviors she asks of her team. Her commitment and discipline kept us moving when things got difficult. In those moments, a team can either turn back or lean in and trust the process. Through her leadership, the team leaned in.

The team at Harman shared our vision for what ACE could become and stayed deeply involved in making it happen. They challenged our thinking and welcomed us to challenge theirs. When we encountered barriers, both teams were willing to examine the work, reconsider familiar approaches, and work through difficult decisions together. I'm grateful for the trust that made those conversations possible.

What we accomplished reflects the contributions of many colleagues across our organizations. It also demonstrates what becomes possible when a client and its service partner take shared ownership of improving how they work together.

The moment that matters most

For all the technology, process design, and performance improvements involved in ACE, the part of Continuous Improvement I find most rewarding is more personal.

It's the moment someone begins to see their own work differently.

Something they have dealt with for years suddenly looks less like an unavoidable part of the job and more like something they have the power to change. They start asking different questions. They notice opportunities in places they used to walk right past. And, often, they bring the next idea to us before we've even finished the last one.

That's the opportunity for our US Continuous Improvement team: to help more people feel equipped and encouraged to discover opportunities, challenge assumptions, and shape better ways of working. Our role is to guide, connect, and build that capability alongside them—not to make improvement dependent on us.

ACE is a strong example of what Lean and digital innovation can accomplish together. The larger opportunity is the mindset it can help spread.

I'll leave you with a question worth carrying into your own day:

How much of your day do you spend working around the work that matters?

What slows you down, interrupts your focus, or creates extra work that pulls you away from where you can add the most value?

Some of those barriers have been around so long that they've become part of how we work. We rarely stop to question them anymore.

Stay Curious. The next time you hear someone say, "But it's always been this way," take the opportunity to ask why.

Could we do it differently? Could we simplify it? Could we eliminate the step altogether?

And when you see an opportunity, share it. Challenge the assumption, bring others into the conversation, and take an active role in making the work better. You have more ability to influence how work gets done than you might realize.

The real jackpot wasn't the process we improved, but the people who discovered they had the power to improve every process they touch next.

Twenty-five questions

01 · What is ACE?

ACE is Arvato Case Execution, a solution built on Dynamics 365 Customer Service and extended with automation, integrations and AI. The technology is the foundation; the real value is how it transforms the way work is performed.

02 · Where did the idea come from?

We saw knowledgeable people spending disproportionate amounts of time gathering information and navigating systems. We asked how we could remove those barriers and free their expertise for activities that create value.

03 · How did you identify customer needs?

Through Learning to See and value stream mapping, we worked alongside clients and operational teams to make inefficiencies visible. We weren't gathering requirements for a tool; we were discovering opportunities to create value.

04 · What role did customer collaboration play?

Customers understand their business, operations understand execution, and CI and digital teams bring methods and technology. Together we can arrive at solutions none of those groups would necessarily have developed independently.

05 · What is a specific example of impact?

Harman vendor compliance went from complex spreadsheet-based research to centralized, guided execution. In July, 1,951 claims were closed in 6.2 days on average despite a 168% increase in volume. August improved to 3.8 days with 100% within the 10-day SLA.

06 · How has employee experience changed?

People spend less time preparing to do their work and more time doing it. Information and guidance are available within the process, making the work more accessible and reducing dependencies on individual experts.

07 · What role does AI play?

AI is one layer of a broader solution. We start with the process, information and decisions, then select the right combination of automation, business rules, integration and AI.

08 · Why Dynamics 365 and Power Platform?

We build on enterprise capabilities instead of reinventing them. Case management, security and extensibility give us a foundation, while Power Platform lets us focus on differentiating capabilities.

09 · What were the biggest challenges?

Understanding complexity, making implicit knowledge explicit and aligning on a better future state. Technology becomes much more effective once we understand the problem.

10 · What was most rewarding personally?

Seeing the excitement when people experience a better way of working. They start identifying opportunities themselves. That is how Continuous Improvement becomes part of the culture.

11 · Can ACE apply to other clients?

Yes, that possibility influenced our design from the beginning. Customer requirements differ, but many underlying capabilities are similar. We want to leverage what we have already learned rather than start from zero.

12 · How does ACE differentiate Arvato?

It demonstrates our ability to work alongside customers as partners in Continuous Improvement and innovation, not simply execute contracted services.

13 · What's next for ACE?

Structured execution creates visibility into recurring issues. For Harman, the opportunity extends from resolving claims more efficiently to learning how to prevent future deductions.

14 · What advice would you give other teams?

Start with the problem. Spend time with people doing the work, understand the barriers, challenge the current state and then determine which technologies can enable a better future state.

15 · Summarize ACE in one sentence.

ACE demonstrates what is possible when Continuous Improvement, our people's expertise, customer collaboration and modern technology come together to fundamentally change the way work is performed.

16 · How did you design for other customers from the start?

We start with the end in mind. We consider reusable functionality, customer-specific logic and portability during design, so future implementations can benefit from earlier investments.

17 · What makes ACE scalable?

Distinguish the scalability of an application from the scalability of its capabilities. Document retrieval, guided research and integration patterns can be adapted without recreating everything.

18 · How important is sharing ACE?

Visibility is one thing; transferability and supportability are another. A complete application may not fit another account, but its components, patterns and lessons can be valuable.

19 · Have we fully capitalized on innovation?

We have tremendous innovation across Arvato, but I believe we have left significant value on the table. Self-service BI, low-code and AI illustrate how distributed capability can lead to duplicated discovery when collaboration does not keep pace.

20 · Is duplication necessarily bad?

No. Separate instances may be required for security, contracts or local needs. What we should challenge is whether the effort to discover and build them needed to be duplicated.

21 · What prevents more effective reuse?

Our structures establish accountability, but budgets, priorities and performance measures are often local. Building for broader reuse may cost one team while benefiting another. We need mechanisms to bridge those boundaries.

22 · What role could the use case library play?

It is a valuable step toward visibility. Its impact could grow by connecting teams during creation and making outcomes genuinely reusable through components, patterns, instructions and lessons learned.

23 · How do you balance customer needs and reuse?

Avoid overengineering. Where broader value is credible, separate business logic from core functionality and design portable components early. Retrofitting portability is often harder.

24 · How does this align with global strategy?

Combine local responsiveness and customer intimacy with collective global capabilities. Scale alone does not guarantee we benefit from our collective knowledge; we must enable its transfer.

25 · What would success look like in five years?

Solving a problem for one customer would naturally strengthen our ability to solve problems for others. Teams would build on existing knowledge and capabilities, allowing innovation to compound.
